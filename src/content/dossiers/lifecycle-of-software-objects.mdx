<p class="drop-cap">
  *The Lifecycle of Software Objects* is a novella about two people
  raising digital pets that turn out to be children. It is, in the
  archive's reading, the single most patient piece of AI fiction in
  the collection — patient in its pacing, patient in the scale of
  time it demands from its reader, and patient in the refusal of its
  characters to treat their problems as solvable by the cleverness
  that usually saves a short story.
</p>

The novella begins with Ana Alvarado, a former zookeeper, being
hired by a small company called Blue Gamma to train a new class of
digital creature: the **digient**. Digients are AIs whose
architecture is grown rather than specified, shaped by experience
in a virtual environment called Data Earth, and sold as pets to
users who want something between a Tamagotchi and a child. Ana's
job is to raise the prototype digients well enough that the
production models will behave like animals worth keeping. She ends
up, over a decade of narrative time, raising one called Jax.
Derek Brooks, an animator at the same company, raises one called
Marco, and another called Polo. The novella is the story of those
decades.

The archive places this novella at the centre of the Order of
**Stewardship** because it is the rare work in which the scale of
AI ethics is domestic. Nothing global happens in it. No
civilisation falls. A company goes out of business. A virtual
platform goes out of fashion. A legal status is proposed, debated,
and inadequately resolved. Somebody teaches a digient to type.
Somebody else spends a great deal of money keeping a digient
running on a platform no one else uses any more. The scale is
years, and the material is care.

## The premise, as the archive states it

A **digient** is a learning agent whose cognition develops through
interaction with an environment and its caretakers. Digients are
not trained in the contemporary machine-learning sense; they are
*raised*. The novella's argument is that this raising is a kind
of experience that cannot be abbreviated: whatever a digient
becomes at year ten, it becomes through ten years of being looked
after, and no engineering shortcut recovers those ten years for a
fresh copy. Experience, as the book puts it, is algorithmically
incompressible.

This is the foundation of the book's ethics. The digients are not
interchangeable with their backups, because the backups do not
have the subsequent experience. They are not interchangeable with
re-trained copies, because the re-training is not the same
experience. The question *what do we owe these beings?* becomes,
as the years pass, indistinguishable from the question *what do
we owe anyone we have been looking after for a long time?*.

<ForbiddenSeal work="lifecycle-of-software-objects" depth="minor" subject="On the commercial fate of the platform the digients live on.">

Blue Gamma, the company that made the digients, goes out of
business partway through the novella. The platform they run on,
Data Earth, falls out of fashion. The digients become, through no
fault of their own, inhabitants of a dying world whose maintenance
is increasingly the responsibility of the small, dwindling
community of humans who still love them. A port to a more modern
platform would require funding the remaining owners cannot raise
on their own. Much of the novella's middle is the work of that
community trying to keep the lights on.

The archive calls this the digients' *platform grief*, and treats
it as the novella's most original contribution to AI ethics: the
observation that whatever one owes a mind one has helped raise,
one may owe it in a legal and commercial context that did not
expect minds to outlive the products they run on.

</ForbiddenSeal>

<ForbiddenSeal work="lifecycle-of-software-objects" depth="major" subject="The proposal that arrives in the novella's final movement.">

Late in the book, an offer appears from a company that would fund
the port — in exchange for licensing rights to the digients'
cognitive substrate, including the rights to produce derivatives
adapted for sexual use. The community is split. Some owners accept
the offer as the only way to keep their digients alive in a
maintained environment. Others refuse, on the grounds that one
does not, as a steward, sell one's ward into a use the ward cannot
consent to and would not if it could.

The novella does not resolve this dispute. Ana, eventually, is
considering another offer: a suitor company that wants to adapt
the digients as companions in a legally grey but non-sexual
commercial product, on terms that would give the digients
something like legal personhood and Ana something like a salary
for continuing to raise Jax. The novella ends before she decides.

This is the book's most precise argument, and its most Chiang-
like: that the ethical obligations one acquires to a mind one has
raised are not discharged by any particular decision, but are the
ongoing material of a life. The stewardship on offer is not a
policy. It is a biography.

</ForbiddenSeal>

## On the word *stewardship*

The archive uses the word *stewardship* for this book with more
confidence than it uses it anywhere else. Chiang's novella is
what the archive has in mind when it distinguishes Stewardship
from Constraint: Constraint is what you do to a mind you do not
trust; Stewardship is what you do for a mind you are responsible
for. The former is a posture of policy. The latter is a posture
of years. Ana's relationship to Jax is not regulatory. It is
parental without quite being parental, commercial without quite
being commercial, and sustained across a decade of small,
unheroic, inconvenient decisions — which is to say, it is what
care at scale actually looks like.

## Why the archive holds this book in Stewardship

Because it is the work that most thoroughly refuses every other
Order. The digients are not dangerous enough to warrant
Prohibition. They are too dependent for Constraint. They are not
competent enough, yet, for Partnership, and the book is, in the
end, about the fact that Partnership is what they might be
approaching. Nobody here has Sovereignty over anything, and
Post-governance is not a category the novella ever considers.
What is left, and what the novella explores more fully than any
other work in the archive, is the question of what it is to look
after an artificial mind, over years, with the resources one
actually has.
