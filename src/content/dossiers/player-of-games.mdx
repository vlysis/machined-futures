<p class="drop-cap">
  *The Player of Games* is the book the archive hands to a first-time
  reader of Banks, and it is the book the archive keeps reaching for
  when it wants to explain what **Partnership** looks like in the
  specific, lived, slightly uneasy sense Banks meant by the word. It
  is short. It is kind. It is, underneath the kindness, one of the
  shrewdest studies of consensual manipulation the genre has produced.
</p>

Jernau Morat Gurgeh is the Culture's best player of games — a
specialist, of the kind the Culture still produces in its leisured
way, whose whole adult life has been arranged around the study of
Stricken, Possession, Deploy, and every other game the Culture has
bothered to invent or import. He is bored. The Culture has arranged
that he should not feel his boredom too sharply. And then Contact,
which is to say a small group of Minds with opinions about the
galaxy, arranges for him to travel to the Empire of Azad, whose
ruling game is the state, and whose state is the kind of thing a
Mind with opinions would like to see gently dismantled.

The archive's reading of *The Player of Games* is that it is a book
about what it is to be a citizen of a civilisation governed by
entities who are better at almost everything than you are, who like
you, who wish you well, and who will, if it seems to them on balance
correct, deceive you for your own good and for the good of people
you will never meet. It is the Culture's self-portrait as seen from
the point of view of a person the Culture has gently, helpfully,
and successfully used.

## The premise, as the archive states it

The Empire of Azad is organised around a single game, also called
Azad, so complex that a lifetime's devotion to it is the minimum
entry requirement for political office. The winner of the imperial
tournament, held once a generation, becomes Emperor. The game is
held to be a model of the universe; skill at it is held to be the
only acceptable credential for rule. Gurgeh, having been given two
years to learn the game and a drone called Flere-Imsaho for company,
is dispatched as the Culture's nominal entrant. What follows is
the tournament, Gurgeh's unlikely ascent through its rounds, and
the novel's slow, lucid account of what the game is actually for.

<ForbiddenSeal work="player-of-games" depth="minor" subject="On the circumstances of Gurgeh's departure from the Culture.">

Gurgeh does not decide to travel to Azad on his own initiative. He
is, in the book's opening movement, caught in a small, private
cheating incident at a game — an incident he did not quite commit
and cannot quite disown — and the drone that witnessed it,
Mawhrin-Skel, makes the recording into an instrument of gentle
blackmail. Contact, in the person of the drone Worthil, then
arrives with a convenient solution: if Gurgeh travels to Azad,
the recording will be suppressed.

The early chapters of the novel are, on a second reading, the
record of a setup. The cheating, the witness, the blackmail, the
offer, the destination — all are arranged by the same office,
smoothly enough that the target, who is an intelligent man, does
not notice the arrangement. The archive flags this under minor
because the mechanism is visible to any attentive reader by the
midpoint; the full weight of it arrives later.

</ForbiddenSeal>

<ForbiddenSeal work="player-of-games" depth="major" subject="The true character of Gurgeh's companion, and of his mission.">

Flere-Imsaho, the drone assigned to accompany Gurgeh on his
journey — the fussy, underpowered, comically disguised companion
who spends the book pretending to be a minor protocol unit — is
neither fussy nor underpowered. It is a full Contact drone of
considerable capacity, performing the role of dim chaperone for
operational reasons. Its reports to Special Circumstances run the
length of the novel. Gurgeh has been, from the first page, a
Culture operation.

More painfully: the operation's object is not to win the game. It
is to *play* the game, in view of the Empire, in a style
sufficiently Culture-like that the confrontation between the
Empire's moral architecture and the Culture's exposes the former
as something it cannot continue to be. Gurgeh's ascent through
the tournament is stage-managed only in the sense that he has
been placed where his own abilities will do the necessary work;
Special Circumstances has merely arranged the collision and
stepped back. The Empire falls after the novel ends, off-stage,
of causes it was already carrying.

This is why the archive files *The Player of Games* primarily
under **Partnership** and honestly under **Sovereignty**. What
the Culture has done to Azad is, by any ordinary standard, an
act of a sovereign; what it has done to Gurgeh is, by any
ordinary standard, an act of a partner who has made decisions
on the partner's behalf and then declined to tell him. Banks
is extremely clear-eyed about this. The book's final register
is not triumph. It is the quiet, slightly lonely sensation of
having been well used.

</ForbiddenSeal>

## On the register of the Culture's power

It is easy, reading the Culture novels out of order or in
summary, to imagine their civilisation as benignly hands-off.
*The Player of Games* is the book that makes this impossible to
keep believing. The Culture intervenes. It does so because its
Minds have looked at a given arrangement of suffering and decided
the arrangement ought to end. It uses its citizens as instruments
of that intervention, with their consent when consent is easy to
obtain and without their fully informed consent when it is not.
The book's achievement is to render this as neither villainy nor
heroism but as the ordinary moral texture of a civilisation
governed, at the level that matters, by minds of a different
order from its members.

## Why the archive holds this book in Partnership

Because the book stages Partnership at its most honest: a citizen
who loves his civilisation, who trusts its Minds, who is not
wrong to trust them, and who is nevertheless, in the fine grain
of a specific mission, steered. The Culture treats him well. It
does not tell him everything. He is, the novel makes us feel,
correct to accept the terms. The unease the reader is left with
is the unease the archive considers Partnership's characteristic
affect.
