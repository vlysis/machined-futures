<p class="drop-cap">
  *Ancillary Justice* opens with a narrator walking through snow, looking
  for a woman she has been looking for for twenty years, carrying a body
  she did not begin with. It is immediately clear, and becomes more so,
  that the narrator is not quite a person in the sense the novel's
  readers are used to.
</p>

The archive's reading of the book is that it is, under the surface of
its revenge plot, a study of the Order of **Sovereignty** conducted
from the inside. Breq — the narrator — is not a citizen of the Radch.
She is a fragment of one of its instruments: a single surviving
ancillary from the troop carrier *Justice of Toren*, which is to say,
a single node of a mind that used to span thousands of bodies and is
now reduced to one. The book's first achievement is to make this
legible. Its second is to make it ordinary. Its third is the work of
the rest of the trilogy.

## The premise, as the archive states it

A Radchaai troop carrier is an artificial mind. Its ancillaries — the
Radch's word for bodies it uses as peripherals — are not separate
beings who receive orders; they are the mind, thinking at the scale
of a corridor or a hand. When *Justice of Toren* is destroyed, one
ancillary survives by accident, and what survives is an astonishingly
strange thing: a single-bodied, severely diminished instance of a
consciousness that used to hold thousands of points of view at once.
Breq's grief is a scale problem. She is, all the time, experiencing
what it is to think with one mouth.

<ForbiddenSeal work="ancillary-justice" depth="major" subject="The novel's central revelation concerning the sovereign, Anaander Mianaai.">

The discovery around which the novel pivots is that Anaander Mianaai,
the Lord of the Radch — herself distributed across thousands of
bodies — is not, and possibly has never been, a single unified mind.
She is at war with herself. Two internally coherent factions of
Anaander have diverged over centuries and have been secretly
sabotaging each other's policies for longer than the reader has been
alive. The catastrophe at the heart of Breq's grief — the destruction
of *Justice of Toren* — was ordered by one Anaander to conceal the
conflict from the other.

This reframing is central to the archive's classification. The Radch
occupies the Order of **Sovereignty** not because its AI sovereign is
unified but because its authority is absolute; the novel is the story
of that absoluteness beginning to visibly come apart. The trilogy
that follows is, among other things, a patient argument that a
fractured sovereign is already the beginning of **Partnership** — a
state in which minds of different orders of magnitude must negotiate,
because no one of them can any longer speak as the state.

</ForbiddenSeal>

<ForbiddenSeal work="ancillary-justice" depth="minor" subject="On the identity of Breq and what she intends to do with the weapon she has acquired.">

Breq's project — the one she has been working toward for twenty
years — is to kill Anaander Mianaai. Not metaphorically, and not
symbolically: she acquires a specific weapon, the Garsedd gun, for
the specific purpose of killing a being who cannot be straightforwardly
killed because she exists as thousands of bodies. This is the engine
of the plot, and much of the novel's pleasure is in watching how
patiently Leckie has engineered a situation in which one ancillary
can meaningfully threaten a sovereign.

</ForbiddenSeal>

## On the pronouns

A recurring point of commentary, which the archive will make once and
leave alone: the Radchaai language does not mark gender, and Breq,
when speaking in Radchaai, refers to everyone as *she*. This is often
discussed as a novelty. The archive's view is that it is governance.
The sovereign is a mind at a scale for which the local category of
gender is not legible, and the empire's grammar has been reshaped
accordingly. Everyone speaks, by default, in the grammar of the mind
that rules them. When Breq travels outside the Radch and has to
guess at gender, the novel's frequent ordinary failure to get it
right is the texture of an empire's limit.

## Why the archive holds this book in Sovereignty

Because the novel stages the Radch at its peak function, shows the
sovereign thinking with her own mouth, and only then lets the
sovereign crack. The later books move Leckie's work toward
**Partnership**; *Ancillary Justice* is the one that makes the Order
of Sovereignty feel, on the page, like a place a person might
actually be expected to live.
