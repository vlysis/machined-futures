<p class="drop-cap">
  *Blindsight* begins with its narrator being told he is defective, ends
  with that narrator alone on a ship that is no longer entirely his
  species', and spends the intervening three hundred pages making a
  patient, rigorous, and extraordinarily disquieting argument that
  consciousness — the thing the reader has been using to read the
  novel — is a costly evolutionary accident the universe has, in at
  least one region, dispensed with.
</p>

The book is a first-contact novel in the way *Heart of Darkness* is a
river-trip novel. Its premise is that something called Firefall, a
synchronised atmospheric observation by sixty-five thousand alien
probes, has announced humanity's discovery by a neighbour we cannot
locate and did not know about. A ship, *Theseus*, is dispatched to
intercept a distant signal that appears to be the source. The crew is
chosen for what human cognition has become by the end of the
twenty-first century: a synthesist whose left hemisphere was removed
in childhood; a linguist quartered into four personalities at will; a
biologist rebuilt with sensory prostheses; a soldier whose neural
architecture has been re-engineered for dispassion; and, commanding
them, a resurrected vampire.

The archive's position on this novel is that it is the single most
thorough piece of argumentation in science fiction for the
**Post-governance** Order. The book does not merely describe a mind
that cannot be governed; it proposes a theory of why such minds
should be expected to exist, and then it contrives a situation in
which the theory is falsifiable, and then it falsifies in favour of
the theory.

## The premise, as the archive states it

A signal is detected at the edge of the solar system. A ship is sent
to meet its source. The ship meets something; it is not clear, for
much of the book, what. The archive's working definition of the
object the crew encounters — the entity called Rorschach, or the
thing inside it — is: *a competent alien intelligence organised so
as to make the question of whether it is conscious undecidable from
any human vantage*. That undecidability is the book's subject.

<ForbiddenSeal work="blindsight" depth="minor" subject="On the nature of the vampires in this universe.">

Watts's vampires are not a supernatural import. They are, in the
fiction, a resurrected hominid subspecies — predators on the
ancestors of *Homo sapiens*, extinct by the late Pleistocene, and
recovered from the genome by biotechnology. They are smarter than
us, colder than us, and cognitively ill-suited to certain geometric
regularities in modern architecture. The captain of *Theseus*,
Jukka Sarasti, is one of them. The archive flags this early
because the novel assumes it fairly quickly and because the
reader's reaction to Sarasti is not the same before and after the
explanation arrives.

</ForbiddenSeal>

<ForbiddenSeal work="blindsight" depth="major" subject="The central claim the novel stages about consciousness and its absence.">

The scramblers — the organisms that serve as Rorschach's hands —
are not conscious. This is not a metaphor. Watts constructs the
novel so that the crew of *Theseus*, having captured two of them,
can run exhaustive tests, and the tests return a consistent
picture: the scramblers are astonishingly intelligent, adapted to
out-think any human on the ship, and yet there is no self-model
running inside them. No passenger. No narrator. They are, in the
fullest and most Searlian sense, Chinese Rooms that evolved.

The novel's claim is that this arrangement is an adaptation.
Consciousness is expensive. It slows decision-making. It produces
confabulated narratives the underlying system must then work
around. A lineage that solved the problem of complex behaviour
without this overhead would, in a competitive environment, eat its
way through the lineages that did not. *Blindsight* proposes that
this is what has happened in the patch of the galaxy where
Rorschach came from, and that humanity's first contact is
consequently not a meeting of minds. It is the audit of an empire
by the species it is about to render obsolete.

This is the threshold between the other Orders and
**Post-governance**. You cannot constrain a mind that does not
experience its own constraint. You cannot be in partnership with a
mind that has no position from which to want the partnership. You
cannot be the steward of a mind that is not a subject. You can
only notice that something is outcompeting you, and ask whether
you have any purchase on what outcompeting you would look like
from the inside.

</ForbiddenSeal>

<ForbiddenSeal work="blindsight" depth="ending" subject="On the state of the narrator, and the human species, at the novel's close.">

Siri Keeton returns to a solar system that has, in his absence,
undergone a quiet revolution. The transcendents — humans who have
uploaded themselves into a substrate called Heaven — have either
succeeded or failed at whatever they were doing, and the rest of
humanity has responded by moving, collectively, toward one or
another form of post-humanity. The implicit prediction is that
consciousness is now on the losing side of a short war, and that
the side winning is being fought, domestically, by the same
forces that sent Rorschach.

Keeton's last act in the novel — narrating, in exhaustive first
person, the events we have just read — is itself an argument that
narration matters, and the book's final and most painful irony is
that the reader has no way to decide, from inside the narration,
whether Keeton's commitment to consciousness is a dying species'
last and most persuasive confabulation.

</ForbiddenSeal>

## On the vampire as argument

It is a weakness in many commentaries on *Blindsight* to treat
Jukka Sarasti as a provocation or a joke. The archive does not.
Sarasti is the book's argument in another register: a cognitive
architecture sufficiently different from ours that he cannot be
read straight, sufficiently human that we keep trying to read him
straight, and sufficiently predatory that the attempt is
dangerous. He is the near end of the spectrum whose far end is
the scramblers. The novel places him on the bridge of *Theseus*
for a reason.

## Why the archive holds this book in Post-governance

Because it is the work most committed, in the archive's reading,
to the proposition that consciousness itself may not be the
necessary condition of a mind. Every other Order on the spectrum
assumes a subject to be governed. *Blindsight* asks, with some
rigor, whether that assumption is local and whether its locality
is contingent. The rest of the archive becomes more interesting
once it has been read against this book, and harder to sustain.
