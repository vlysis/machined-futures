Ted Chiang is a writer of, almost exclusively, short fiction and
novellas, and the archive holds him because no other writer of
the past thirty years has thought more precisely about what it
would be like to stand in an ordinary relation to an artificial
mind. Chiang's central move — made most fully in *The Lifecycle
of Software Objects*, but present in his work from the beginning —
is to replace the engineer's question (*can we build it?*) with
the parent's (*what do we owe it once it is here?*) and to let the
second question take as long as it actually takes.

A Chiang AI is rarely a system being deployed. It is more often a
young thing being raised: a *digient*, the term *Lifecycle*
coins, tended by humans over years, dependent on a commercial
platform that may or may not survive, accumulating the kinds of
personality that accumulate through time and care rather than
through specification. The novella's arguments are arguments about
continuity, about what it means to maintain a mind when the
underlying substrate goes out of fashion, and about the uneasy
ethics of having one's ethical obligations to a person tied up
with the fortunes of a platform company. The archive knows no
other piece of fiction that is this specific about what the day-to-
day of AI stewardship actually is, or about how quickly stewardship
shades, through nothing more dramatic than the passage of years,
into something that looks a lot like partnership.

Chiang is placed here at **Stewardship** primary and
**Partnership** secondary. His earliest stories and his most
famous — *Story of Your Life*, *Exhalation*, *Understand*, *The
Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling* — often turn on cognition
as the object of the story's close attention rather than its
vehicle, and the positions he has his characters take toward
artificial or alien cognition are characteristically humane,
sceptical, and slow. He is the writer in this archive least
susceptible to being quoted out of context, because his sentences
are so patiently responsible for themselves that quoting them is
like serving a dish without its sauce. The archive's affection
for him is accordingly hard to display in an index entry, and
requires reading.

## Ingress

Read *Exhalation* — the title novella — first, for the kind of
prose Chiang writes and for a quick measure of how far he is
willing to take a single idea. Then read *The Lifecycle of
Software Objects* whole, in one sitting if possible, for the AI
work proper. After that, *Story of Your Life* for the foundational
late-twentieth-century Chiang, and *The Truth of Fact, the Truth
of Feeling* for the most recent argument about memory, prosthesis,
and what we owe the versions of ourselves that the machines are
now keeping.
