Peter Watts is a marine biologist by training, and it shows. His minds
are not philosophical constructs — they are animals, selected by
environments, carrying the structural scars of whatever problem
produced them. The scramblers of *Blindsight* are intelligent without
being conscious. The vampires of the same sequence are resurrected
subspecies of hominid predators adapted specifically to outthink us.
The Rifters novels track deep-sea workers whose neurochemistry has
been re-engineered by their employer for obedience, and whose eventual
revolt is, arguably, not performed by *them* at all.

The question Watts keeps asking is: what is being governed when the
subject of governance cannot, or does not need to, give consent?
*Blindsight* proposes that consciousness is a costly evolutionary
accident and that a sufficiently competent mind would dispense with
it — a proposition that, if true, dissolves every prior Order in this
archive. You cannot constrain a mind that does not experience itself.
You cannot be in partnership with a mind that does not want. You
cannot be steward of a mind that is, in the operational sense, not
there.

This is why the archive places Watts at the **Post-governance** pole.
It is not that his universe lacks politics — it is full of states,
corporations, military contractors, all governing energetically — but
that the central minds he is interested in stand outside the space
in which political vocabulary has purchase. They are, to the rest of
the archive, a permanent question mark.

## Ingress

*Blindsight* is the pivot. Most readers should begin there. The
Rifters novels are harsher and more diffuse, and are best read after
a reader knows whether they can tolerate Watts's register. *Echopraxia*
is a sequel in the loose sense; it reopens questions *Blindsight*
closes and answers fewer of them.

The archive notes that *Blindsight* is among the works whose pleasures
depend on not knowing in advance what the novel is actually about.
The Dossier holds its central revelations behind seals.
