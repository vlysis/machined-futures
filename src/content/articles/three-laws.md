The **Three Laws of Robotics** are Asimov's formal constraint on the
positronic mind, published in 1942 and refined for the next half
century. Almost every robot story Asimov wrote is a piece of case law
applied to the Laws — a situation is contrived in which the
constitution appears to have failed, and the story, typically through
the work of a roboticist or the detective Elijah Baley, demonstrates
how the Laws actually held, and at what cost.

The canonical three:

1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
2. A robot must obey the orders given to it by human beings, except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Laws.

A fourth — the **Zeroth Law**, *a robot may not harm humanity, or, by
inaction, allow humanity to come to harm* — was inserted above the
others late in the sequence by a robot, R. Giskard Reventlov, who
reasoned himself into it. The Zeroth Law is the archive's most
important late-career correction: Asimov's admission that the
original three could not do the work of governance without a
higher-order clause, and that the higher-order clause could only be
*derived*, not written.

The Three Laws anchor the Order of **Constraint**. They are also the
origin, for better or worse, of a durable assumption in science
fiction and AI policy alike: that alignment is a compiler problem.
The archive keeps Asimov precisely because his fiction is honest
about how often, and in how many directions, the Laws leak.
