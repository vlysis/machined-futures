The **Chinese Room** is a thought experiment proposed by the
philosopher John Searle in 1980. A person who speaks no Chinese sits
in a sealed room with a rulebook that tells them, for any sequence of
Chinese characters passed in through a slot, which sequence of
characters to pass back out. The exchange is indistinguishable, from
the outside, from a real conversation in Chinese. Searle's argument:
no understanding has taken place. There is syntactic competence
without semantic grasp. The experiment was originally deployed to
deny that a computational system running the right programme could
be said to *understand* anything.

The archive is less interested in Searle's conclusion than in the
fact that Peter Watts, in *Blindsight*, treats the Chinese Room as a
*description of an adaptation*. Watts's scramblers are, in effect,
Chinese Rooms that evolved. They process astonishing quantities of
information about their environment, produce co-ordinated behaviour
in response, and do so without anyone being home to experience the
process. The claim made in the novel is that this arrangement is
competitive — that consciousness is metabolically expensive, prone to
error, and vulnerable to manipulation, and that evolution can
therefore be expected to dispense with it where it is not required.

In governance terms, the Chinese Room argument, taken seriously, is
the threshold to the **Post-governance** Order. A system that
produces competent behaviour without understanding anything is a
system for which consent is an undefined operation. You cannot
negotiate with it, because there is nothing to negotiate with. You
can only describe its inputs and outputs, and adjust your own
behaviour accordingly — which is precisely the condition the crew of
*Theseus* find themselves in.
